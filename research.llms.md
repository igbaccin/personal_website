### Living Standards in Ghana, 1955–2005

I. Martins

*Labour and Work in Ghana Since Independence* · Edited by Gareth Austin, Akua Britwum, Lamine Doumbia, Andreas Eckert, and Nana Yaw Sapong. London: Bloomsbury Academic

Using consumption baskets matched to the diets of Ghana’s three ecological zones, I show that national baskets understate living standards in the northern savannah, and that the widening gap between regions in 1988–2005 came mainly from rural areas.

Abstract

This paper constructs regional welfare ratios for Ghana from 1955 to 2005, combining five rounds of the Ghana Living Standards Survey with archival wage and price data. Using Allen-type consumption baskets calibrated to the dietary patterns of Ghana’s three ecological zones, I show that national baskets systematically misrepresent regional living standards in countries with significant dietary heterogeneity. Under a national basket priced at regional levels, several northern regions appear unable to meet subsistence. When the basket composition is allowed to reflect zone-specific diets, welfare ratios improve substantially in the savannah regions, where local staples (millet, sorghum) are cheaper than the cassava and plantain that dominate the national basket. I further decompose regional welfare by urban and rural status, showing that both urban and rural areas are diverging across regions, but that rural divergence is far more severe. The aggregate widening of regional inequality in Ghana over 1988–2005 is driven primarily by the rural sector.

### Från arbetskraft till kapital: Slaveriets ekonomiska logik i globalt perspektiv

I. Martins and E. Green

*Statsvetenskaplig tidskrift*

Enslaved people could serve as collateral as early as classical Athens and Rome, and in nineteenth-century Brazil and the Cape Colony courts and foreclosure made slave-backed lending routine, so emancipation also wiped out collateral that creditors relied on.

Abstract

Slavery is usually analysed as an extreme form of labour coercion. This article treats it instead as a property regime over persons that simultaneously organises labour and creates alienable assets that can be valued, transferred, and pledged. I distinguish feasible collateralisation from systemic collateralisation: the former requires enforceable remedies, the latter requires routinised instruments and repeatable enforcement channels that make slave property legible to third parties. Evidence from classical Athens and Rome shows that enslaved people could serve as collateral without modern registries, separating feasibility from systemic use. Comparative case studies of nineteenth-century Brazil and the Cape Colony trace when slave-backed lending became routine, highlighting the roles of creditor remedies, foreclosure, courts, and market liquidity. The findings reframe the slavery and capitalism debate and interpret emancipation as a shock to collateral and creditor protection, not only to labour relations.

### [Capital and Coercion: Slavery after the 1807 Import Ban in the Cape Colony](https://doi.org/10.1093/ereh/heag012)

I. Martins

*European Review of Economic History* · heag012

After Britain banned slave imports in 1807, wealthier households in Stellenbosch and Graaff-Reinet kept accumulating enslaved people, holding them as capital assets in a colony where capital was scarce.

[Replication package ↗](https://www.icpsr.umich.edu/sites/icpsr/view/studies/237703)

Abstract

The 1807 Slave Trade Act banned slave imports across the British Empire, triggering a sharp supply shock. Using newly digitised tax censuses from the Cape Colony, this article examines slave accumulation patterns in Stellenbosch and Graaff-Reinet following the supply shock. Despite stark ecological and institutional differences, both districts show similar post-abolition trajectories. This challenges models linking coercion to land–labour ratios, supervision costs, or frontier openness. Testing five frameworks, the paper finds the strongest support for the view of slaves as capital assets. Wealthier households continued accumulating slaves, suggesting slavery persisted not only as labour but as an asset strategy amid capital scarcity.

### [Theoretical Foundations of the Economics of Slavery: Enslaved People as Capital Investments in the Atlantic World](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1740022825100296)

I. Martins and E. Green

*Journal of Global History* · 21(1), 22–40

Comparing the United States, Brazil and the Cape Colony, we show that enslavers held enslaved people as both labour and capital investment, and that this investment role is key to explaining why slavery persisted.

Abstract

Despite the prevalence of slavery in world history, our understanding of its persistence remains limited. Most previous studies focus primarily on slavery as a labour contract, indistinguishable from other coercive arrangements such as serfdom. More recent literature on slavery in the United States shows that enslaved people also played an important role as financial instruments. In this article, we extend the investigation by comparing slavery in the United States with that in Brazil and the Cape Colony. We show that despite significant geographic, demographic, and economic differences, slavery was not merely a labour arrangement in the three cases but a unique institution that gave enslavers complete rights over mobile property. Slavery provided access to both labour and capital, with the capital investment dimension being key to understanding its persistence. We argue that understanding slavery’s persistence requires recognizing enslaved people as both sources of labour and capital investment.

### [Is the business of conquest the conquest of business?](https://www.thecasecentre.org/products/view?id=208126)

I. Martins

*The Case Centre* · 225-0045-1

Students take the seats of the Heeren XVII, the VOC’s governing board, and decide where the company should expand next, weighing profit against the political and ethical costs of conquest.

Abstract

This case explores the interplay between commerce and colonial expansion through the lens of the Dutch East India Company (VOC) in the 17th century. Tasked with securing profit while navigating geopolitical rivalries, the VOC’s decision-makers faced dilemmas that blurred the lines between trade and conquest. This case places students in the role of the Heeren XVII, the governing board of the VOC, requiring them to evaluate where the company should expand next. While commercial logic dictates efficiency and profitability, expansion carries unforeseen ethical, social, and political consequences. The case challenges students to grapple with decision-making under uncertainty, much like historical actors did, without the benefit of hindsight. By analyzing economic incentives, risk assessment, and ethical dilemmas, students reflect on the historical realities of corporate-driven expansion and its modern parallels. Ultimately, the case asks whether commercial ambition inevitably leads to political dominance and whether business leaders bear responsibility for the long-term consequences of their decisions. Designed for business, history, and political economy courses, this case encourages debate on globalization, ethics, and the power dynamics of trade.

### [Fertility responses to short-term economic stress: Price volatility and wealth shocks in a pre-transitional settler colony](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eeh.2024.101620)

J. Cilliers, M. Mariotti, and I. Martins

*Explorations in Economic History* · 101620

Cape settler households that lost slave wealth at emancipation in 1834 appear to have postponed births, while price volatility had no clear effect on conceptions.

[Replication package ↗](https://www.icpsr.umich.edu/sites/icpsr/view/studies/208362)

Abstract

This paper examines the effects of short-term economic stress, captured by general price volatility and a negative wealth shock on short-run fertility behavior in the rural pre-transitional society of the Cape Colony. First, we link complete birth histories of settler women from the South African Families database to consumer price index data to examine the effect of price volatility on conceptions. Next, we link the same birth histories to slave owner and slave emancipation data to examine the effect of a negative wealth shock on conception. Upon slave emancipation in 1834, former slave owners received on average only between 40 and 50 % of the market value of their slaves as compensation, resulting in a substantial reduction in their wealth. Relying on event history models that look simultaneously at stopping and spacing, we do not find strong evidence in support of fertility control in response to general price volatility. We do find greater variance in birth interval lengths for former slaveholding households during and immediately after emancipation, suggesting that a negative wealth shock is associated with increased fertility limitation through postponement in this context.

### [Resilience to shrinking as a catch-up strategy: a comparison of Brazil and Indonesia, 1964–2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-023-09392-1)

T. Axelsson and I. Martins

*Studies in Comparative International Development* · 59(3), 491–516

We argue that differences in structural transformation, autonomy and inclusion explain why Indonesia has weathered economic shrinking better than Brazil since 1964.

Abstract

Development economics has long focused on growth patterns to explain countries’ ability to catch up and forge ahead. We argue, however, that resilience to economic shrinking matters more. Using the examples of Brazil and Indonesia, we propose that a framework consisting of social capabilities—namely structural transformation, autonomy, and inclusion—can explain why Indonesia is more resilient to economic shrinking than Brazil and why the country is more likely to be successful in its catching-up process.

### [Inequality, poverty, and resilience to economic shrinking](https://doi.org/10.1108/IJDI-06-2023-0168)

A. Smythe, I. Martins, and M. Andersson

*International Journal of Development Issues* · 23(1), 40–81

Across 23 developing countries over 42 years, falling poverty is associated with fewer episodes of economic shrinking, while changes in income inequality show no such relationship.

Abstract

**Purpose.** With the recognition that generating economic growth is not the same as sustaining it, the challenge to catch-up and growth literature is discerning between these processes. Recent research suggests that the decline in the frequency of “shrinking” episodes is more important for long-term development than higher growth rates. By using a framework centred around social capabilities, this study aims to investigate the effects of income inequality and poverty on economic shrinking frequency, as opposed to previous literature that has exclusively had a growth focus. The aim is to investigate how and why some societies might be more resilient to economic shrinking.

**Design/methodology/approach.** The research is a quantitative study, and the authors build a longitudinal data set including 23 developing countries throughout 42 years to test the paper’s purpose. This study uses country and period fixed-effects specifications as well as cross-sectional graphical representations to investigate the relationship between proxies of economic inclusivity and the frequency of shrinking episodes.

**Findings.** The authors demonstrate that while inclusive societies are more resilient to shrinking overall, it is changes in poverty levels, but not changes in income inequality, that appear to be correlated with economic shrinking frequency. Inequality, while still an important element to explain countries’ growth potential as an initial condition, does not seem to make the sample more resilient to shrinking. The authors conclude that the mechanisms in which poverty and inequality are correlated with the catch-up process must run through different channels. Ultimately, processes that explain growth may intersect but not always overlap with the ones that explain resilience to shrinking.

**Originality/value.** The need for inclusive growth in long-term development has been championed for decades, yet inclusion has seldom been explored from the shrinking perspective. Though poverty reduction is already an important mainstream political objective, this paper differentiates itself by providing an alternate viewpoint of why this is important. Income inequality could have more of an economic growth limiting effect, while poverty reduction could be required to build resilience to economic shrinking. Developing countries will need both growth and resilience to shrinking, to catch-up with higher-income economies, which policymakers might need to balance carefully.

### [An age of disentangled research?](https://doi.org/10.58875/WBCQ3772)

I. Martins and S. Schwaag Serger

*Issues in Science and Technology* · 40(1), 38–43

Coauthorship between Chinese and US researchers has begun to decline, and we ask what growing political constraints mean for international science.

Abstract

This article examines the emerging decline in international research collaboration, especially between China and the United States, and asks whether we are entering a new era of globally fragmented science. While US–China coauthorship is decreasing, European trends are more uneven. Drawing on bibliometric data and institutional analysis, the authors argue that growing political constraints and institutional caution are shifting global scientific cooperation into a more divided, uncertain phase, raising urgent questions about the future of science as a public good.

### [Legacies of loss: The health outcomes of slaveholder compensation in the British Cape Colony](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eeh.2022.101506)

I. Martins, J. Cilliers, and J. Fourie

*Explorations in Economic History* · 101506

Slaveholders who lost more wealth through the partial compensation of 1834 lived slightly shorter lives, an effect concentrated among older owners; their children and grandchildren were unaffected.

[Replication package ↗](https://www.icpsr.umich.edu/sites/icpsr/view/studies/183526)

Abstract

Can wealth shocks have intergenerational health consequences? We use the partial compensation slaveholders received after the 1834 slave emancipation in the British Cape Colony to measure the intergenerational effects of a wealth loss on longevity. We find that a greater loss of slave wealth shortened the lifespans of the generation of slaveholders that experienced the shock albeit these effects are usually small and mostly confined to older cohorts of slaveholders who likely exploited slaves both as labor and capital inputs. The lifespans of those of the second generation who survived infancy were unaffected by the shortfalls and no effects of the shortfall were found for the third generation.

### [Collateral Effect: Slavery and Wealth in the Cape Colony](https://portal.research.lu.se/en/publications/collateral-effect-slavery-and-wealth-in-the-cape-colony)

I. Martins

*Lund University* · PhD thesis

Without secure title to land or organised credit, Cape slaveholders used enslaved people as financial assets to raise capital, which helps explain how slavery worked and persisted in the colony.

Abstract

This thesis reassesses the framework we have come to accept around the dynamics of slavery in a series of papers which, together, shed new light on the economics of coercion. Employing a range of newly digitized historical databases covering the economic life and genealogical history of the British Cape Colony through the 18th and 19th centuries, it explores the determinants of labor coercion in light of two significant institutional shocks: the Slave Trade Act 1807, when the transshipment of slaves became illegal, and the Slave Abolition Act 1833 when the possession of slaves was outlawed. Slaveholding households are analyzed from a longitudinal perspective, such that the interdependencies of slave ownership, agricultural output, and related capital are considered simultaneously. It concludes that scenarios with weak property rights to land and lack of organized means for the provision of credit rendered slaves a suitable financial instrument allowing slaveholders to exploit the enslaved as means to raise capital beyond the agricultural labor. It magnifies the importance of slavery in the colony and provides economic historians with another tool to further interpret the profitability and the efficiency of the slave system. Understanding the proprietary relationship between masters and slaves and, consequently, their exploitation beyond agricultural work is key to explaining the dynamics of slavery in its entirety.

### [What caused poverty reduction in Brazil during the 2000s: sectoral growth or public expenditures](https://revistas.uexternado.edu.co/index.php/oasis/article/view/6232/8773)

A. Palacio and I. Martins

*OASIS. Observatorio de Análisis de los Sistemas Internacionales* · 31, 185–213

Growth in services, led by public administration, did more than agriculture to reduce poverty in Brazil in the 2000s, and state and municipal spending on human capital did more than federal cash transfers.

Abstract

We ask what caused poverty to decline in Brazil during the first decade of the 21st century. Our contribution lies in the introduction of a structural change perspective to assess the evolution of poverty by considering the sectoral impact of growth and the social policies at the federal, state and municipal level. By structural change we mean the recomposition of output and employment over time. We run a first difference model to estimate the effects of mean income per capita by sector and of disaggregated public expenditures, without any attempts to infer causality. We confirm previous findings in the literature that the service sector rather than agriculture contributes the most to the sustained poverty reduction. Strikingly, the public administration is the leading sub-sector. We also find that state and municipal expenditures in human capital contribute more to poverty reduction than federal expenditures associated with conditional cash transfer programs; investment in infrastructure does not seem to contribute to poverty reduction. In short, we conclude that the payoffs of decentralized policies associated with human capital can be seen in the short run and therefore raise the bar for politicians to maintain and care for these policies. Furthermore, the public service sector, which is one of the main employers in today’s economy, must find ways to innovate and improve productivity if poverty reduction is to be sustainable in the long run.

### The Case for Artisanal Doctoral Supervision

I. Martins

*Studies in Higher Education* · Revise and resubmit

Universities track doctoral students through study plans and progress reviews. I propose revisable plans and brief records of reasons, so that supervisors remain accountable and students can still change course when new evidence calls for it.

Abstract

Universities increasingly organise doctoral supervision through study plans and progress reviews. These instruments protect students by making promised support verifiable. They also establish an early account of the research against which later progress is judged. In humanities research, new evidence may unsettle that account, and a change that improves the thesis can then look like a failure to keep to the plan. This conceptual article brings together research on doctoral supervision and research on how universities assess academic work to develop a typology of supervision. It distinguishes structure, which makes support reliable, from judgement, which helps students decide what a discovery means for their thesis. Their combination produces nominal, unstructured, bureaucratic, and artisanal supervision. Bureaucratic supervision combines reliable support with weak judgement and suits reviews that measure progress against the plan. Artisanal supervision uses reliable structure to make room for justified changes to the thesis within the time a doctorate allows. The article argues that formal records capture intellectual judgement poorly and can steer supervision towards what they record. It proposes revisable plans and brief, protected records of reasons, so that universities can hold supervision to account while leaving room for discovery.

### Retrieval-Restricted Reasoning: Supporting Scholarly Synthesis with Language Models

I. Martins

*Historical Methods: A Journal of Quantitative and Interdisciplinary History* · Revise and resubmit

I built a method that lets language models write literature reviews only from sources the researcher supplies; across 100 test reviews with 4,767 citations, 99 contained none of the five measured citation errors.

[Replication package ↗](https://www.icpsr.umich.edu/sites/icpsr/view/studies/246321)

Abstract

Large language models can synthesize scholarship quickly, while the relation between their prose and the underlying sources often remains difficult to verify. This paper introduces Retrieval-Restricted Reasoning (RRR), an architecture for model-assisted synthesis over a researcher-defined corpus. RRR admits page-level evidence, binds it to stable identifiers before composition, renders citations from those records, and records a refusal when the collection cannot sustain the question. I evaluate the architecture through repeated runs on a contested economic-history literature. Across 100 full-RRR runs, the reviews contained 4,767 parsed citations. Ninety-nine reviews were free of the five measured error classes. One review contained a single citation identity that did not resolve to the declared collection; the audit found no invalid page, citation-format, or quotation error. A corpus-only instruction reduced boundary crossing substantially, and page-based comparison conditions showed that traceability arose through a layered contract of retrieval, evidence binding, rendering, writer intervention, and final checking. Model and workflow comparisons further separated writing capability from the preservation of a checkable source path. RRR supports historical synthesis by keeping claims connected to inspectable scholarship while leaving the researcher to judge the corpus and the historical interpretations built from it.

### [Asset ownership turns climate exposure into inequality](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-9859396/v1)

I. Martins, S. Grab, and E. Green

*Nature Communications* · Revise and resubmit

Linking weather to 25,000 household tax records, we show that wealthier households more often owned the commodities that seasonal weather favoured, and years when weather raised returns to those commodities were years of wider inequality in movable assets.

[Read preprint ↗](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-9859396/v1) [Replication package ↗](https://www.icpsr.umich.edu/sites/icpsr/view/studies/248487)

Abstract

As climate change alters weather risk, wealth is often treated as a buffer after shocks occur. We show that wealth can shape the economic shock before it arrives by determining which assets households own. In a pre-industrial agrarian society, we link weather to 25,000 household tax records and commodity prices. Households held different production bundles, and the same seasonal weather could raise returns to one bundle while lowering returns to another. Wealthier households more often owned the commodities favoured by observed weather variation, and prices did not systematically offset these differences. These portfolio differences predict next-year changes in movable assets. Years in which weather raised returns to commodities disproportionately owned by wealthier households were years of wider movable-asset inequality. Wealth determined whether weather became loss or gain.

### Slave Emancipation and Agricultural Output in the Cape Colony

I. Martins

*Cliometrica* · Submitted

After emancipation, Stellenbosch farms whose compensation fell further short of their slaves’ assessed value lost more wine and grain output, especially large mixed producers, consistent with the loss of slave property used in lending.

[Replication package ↗](https://www.icpsr.umich.edu/sites/icpsr/view/studies/248667)

Abstract

Slave emancipation reorganised labour and removed a legally protected form of wealth that had supported collateralised credit. Using a panel of farmers in Stellenbosch, Cape Colony, from 1824 to 1844, this study links annual tax censuses to slave valuations and compensation awards. The compensation gap is the difference between the local valuation and the cash award. It measures the portion of assessed slave value that compensation did not replace. The estimates compare each farm’s output before and after emancipation and ask whether output changed more on farms with larger compensation gaps, after accounting for changes shared by all farms and for recorded labour, household size, and livestock. After emancipation, grain and wine output declined more on farms with larger compensation gaps, especially among large mixed producers. Among these farms, every additional £10 in the compensation gap per enslaved person is associated with losses equal to 12 percent of earlier wine output and 20 percent of earlier grain output. This pattern, together with historical evidence on the use of slave property in lending, is consistent with a balance sheet channel operating alongside labour reorganisation.

### Democracy and Rural Inequality in Kenya, 1997–2010

J. Makuei and I. Martins

*African Affairs* · Submitted

Across 31 Kenyan constituencies from 1997 to 2010, rural households gained better access to fertiliser, roads and markets after the first democratic transfer of power, while the gap in household wealth between constituencies barely changed.

Abstract

Democratic transitions can expand access to rural infrastructure and productive inputs. It remains unclear whether they can reduce entrenched inequalities in household wealth. We examine this issue in the context of Kenya’s first inter-party transfer of presidential power. We combine electoral outcomes in 31 constituencies with five rounds of rural household surveys from 1997 to 2010. Fertiliser use and road and market access improved over time. In contrast, the spatial distribution of household assets was far more stable: constituencies that were initially advantaged stayed so. Moreover, we find no subsequent economic or public programme benefits for constituencies that elected Mwai Kibaki as president in 2007. Kenya illustrates a key challenge facing Africa’s new democracies. While access can improve over a decade, entrenched inequality can persist.

### Held by the Elite: Monopoly Rights and Family Fortunes at the Cape of Good Hope, 1680–1794

I. Martins, J. Schoots, and E. Green

The Dutch East India Company auctioned the right to sell alcohol at the Cape every year. The settlers who won these rights already owned enslaved people more often than others, and those who ran them as a business accumulated more, while their families lasted no longer than other settler families.

Abstract

Many accounts of early modern elites hold that the privileges states sold made their holders rich and their families lasting. We test this for the alcohol retail rights (\textit{pachten}) that the Dutch East India Company auctioned each year at the Cape of Good Hope, 1680–1794, following every holder through auction records, an annual household census and a genealogical register, against settlers who never held a \textit{pacht}. A core of established operators kept their lots, paying at least as much as outsiders. Future holders already owned enslaved people more often than other settlers, and Cape-born ones had married into \textit{pachter} families three and a half times as often as other Cape-born men. Operators who held intensively from the start accumulated enslaved people, and one-year holders show no measurable change. \textit{Pachter} families lasted no longer than comparable settler families and married among themselves no more often. Sold every year at open auction, the privilege consolidated the position of slaveholding households and rewarded those who ran it as a business, without founding a lasting elite.

### Catch-up and Resilience to Shrinking, 1890–2019

A. Smythe and I. Martins

Across 105 countries since 1955, productivity and inputs often moved in opposite directions, and a country’s earlier record of growing and shrinking helps predict its later expansion.

Abstract

Growth depends on both gains in expansion and losses in contraction. We present a production-focused account of long-term economic growth and shrinking. We disaggregate shifts in the production function from movements along the production function, to provide a reconciliation of traditional growth accounting and the growing-and-shrinking framework. This synthesis exposes production patterns hidden behind overall growth. We find shifts in productivity and changes in input levels frequently move in opposite directions for a sample of 105 countries over the period 1955–2019. To illustrate the role of outside factors, we contrast production responses to an increase versus decrease in the global prices of a country’s exports. A decline in prices is followed by a backward movement along the production function, primarily through a lasting fall in capital services per capita. We examine whether decadal production histories contain clues for future catch-up. Patterns in 10-year production histories that originated between 1964 and 1989 select countries with significantly greater expansionary gains in the 1990s and 2000s. The earlier record of growing and shrinking also improves predictions of later expansion beyond the information contained in standard growth accounting.
